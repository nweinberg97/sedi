The big idea

We are all intuitive psychologists

We constantly guess at the causes of other people's behavior, and those guesses shape how we treat them. Heider noticed we lean toward explaining others by who they are rather than the situation they're in. Later researchers named that tilt the fundamental attribution error.2,3

Three lessons

1

Naive psychology

Ordinary people have working theories about each other's minds. Heider took those theories seriously as a subject of study.2

2

We see intentions everywhere

In an earlier study with Marianne Simmel, people watching moving shapes described them as having goals and personalities.2,4

3

Relationships seek balance

We prefer our likes and dislikes to fit together, and feel tension when they don't.2

The framework

Attribution and Balance2

Two of Heider's ideas still used everywhere from design to diplomacy.2

1

Attribution

Person or situation?

We explain behavior by something inside a person (traits, motives) or outside (circumstances), and over-weight the inside for others.2,3

Try: Before judging, list situational reasons.
2

Balance

We like consistency

My friend's friend should be my friend. When a relationship triangle is out of balance, we feel pressure to fix it.2

Try: Map the triangle to see what's really in tension.

From the book

Tools to try

The attribution flip

Applying Heider's attribution idea.2,3

  1. Write your explanation of someone's annoying behavior.
  2. Write three situational explanations.
  3. Act on the most charitable one that's still plausible.
  4. Then ask, don't assume.

The balance triangle

Applying Heider's balance idea.2

  1. Draw a triangle: you, another person, and an issue.
  2. Mark each link + or −.
  3. Find the link causing tension, and decide whether to change a view or accept the difference.
