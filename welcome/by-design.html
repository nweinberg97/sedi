Lesson 01 · Behavior design

Tiny Habits

BJ Fogg, PhD · 2019

The Small Changes That Change Everything: a practical method for changing behavior by starting very small, from the founder of Stanford's Behavior Design Lab. 1,2

BJ Fogg, PhD, founded the Behavior Design Lab at Stanford University. 2

🌱Tiny HabitsBJ Fogg, PhD

Principle

Design for low motivation

Motivation rises and falls through the day, in what Fogg calls the Motivation Wave. When we're tired, it's the first thing to go. A behavior designed to work on your lowest-energy day is far more likely to happen: make it easy, make it visible, anchor it, and make it feel good. 1,4

Framework

The Fogg Behavior Model1,3

B = MAP

A Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt come together at the same moment. To design for a behavior, pull on those three levers. 3

1

Motivation1,4

How much you want to do it

Your desire to do the behavior. It's real but unreliable, so Fogg advises matching yourself with behaviors you already want rather than trying to manufacture motivation. 1,4

How to design for it

  • Celebrate right after the behavior so it feels good. Fogg calls that feeling Shine 5
  • Choose behaviors people already want to do 1
Physical

Confetti at the marathon finish line

Digital

A badge after someone's first contribution

Experience

Stairs that play a note with every step

2

Ability1,6

How easy it is to do

Fogg's Ability Chain: time, money, physical effort, mental effort and fit with your routine. The weakest link sets how hard the behavior feels. 6

How to design for it

  • Make it less hard: remove the weakest link, or make it the default 6
  • Make it tiny: use a Starter Step (just the first move) or scale it back to a smaller version 6
Physical

Seats in small circles so talking is natural

Digital

A one-tap poll instead of a blank reply box

Experience

One-word intros to break the ice

3

Prompt1,7,8

What reminds you to do it, right now

No behavior happens without a prompt. Fogg names three kinds: Person prompts (a feeling or body signal), Context prompts (something in your surroundings) and Action prompts (a routine you already do). Action prompts, which he calls Anchors, are the most reliable. 7,8

How to design for it

  • Anchor it: attach the new behavior right after an existing routine 7
  • Make it visible: put the cue where it can't be missed 1
Physical

A sign on the café door: “Joining the run?”

Digital

“How do you want to show up today?” right after login

Experience

Grab a coloured badge, find someone with the same colour

Note: Motivation is the least reliable lever. When designing, start with ability and prompts. 1,4

System

The Tiny Design Test By Design

Run any idea through five levers drawn from Fogg's model.

  1. Make it easyShrink the action or lower the stakes. 6
  2. Make it less hardRemove the weakest link in the Ability Chain. 6
  3. Make it visiblePut context prompts in view. 7
  4. Anchor a promptTie it to something people already do. 7
  5. Celebrate completionMake finishing feel good straight away. 5

Example: Café run club

  • Easy: shorten the run from 5 km to 1 km
  • Less hard: a QR code on the door for quick sign-up
  • Anchor: the sign on the exit reminds people as they leave
  • Visible: posters show the start time, route and social fun
  • Celebrate: a big group cheer at the finish

Reflect

How will you use this model to shape behavior for the better?

How could you design for joy? For calm? For connection?

Practice 30 min1,6,7

  1. Choose one outcome to design for: joy, connection, calm, movement…
  2. Write a Tiny Habits recipe for it: “After I [anchor], I will [tiny behavior].” Then decide how you'll celebrate.
  3. Run the idea through one lever of the Tiny Design Test.
  4. Share your design with someone and ask where they'd still feel friction.

Lesson 02 · Attention

Indistractable

Nir Eyal with Julie Li · 2019

How to Control Your Attention and Choose Your Life: a guide to understanding distraction and designing your habits, calendar and surroundings for focus. 1,2,5

Nir Eyal writes about the intersection of psychology, technology and business, and is also the author of Hooked. 3

🎯IndistractableNir Eyal with Julie Li

Principle

Design to hack distraction

Eyal's starting point is that distraction is mostly an escape from discomfort, not a technology problem. External triggers then hijack attention unless your environment, tools and rituals are designed on purpose. Distraction isn't fixed by willpower. It's fixed by design. 3,4

Framework

The Indistractable Model3,4

Traction is any action that pulls you toward what you intend; distraction pulls you away. Eyal's model has four parts. 3,4

1

Master internal triggers3,4

Deal with the discomfort underneath

Boredom, loneliness, worry and fatigue are what push us to distraction. 3

How to design for it

  • Notice the feeling and write it down before acting on it 3
  • Use the 10-minute rule: wait ten minutes before giving in 3,6
  • Surf the urge until it passes, a technique Eyal draws from Jonathan Bricker's research 4
  • Reimagine the task to find novelty in it, and reimagine your temperament, since willpower doesn't simply run out 4
Your day

“I'm bored” noted on a card, then back to work

Your work

Ten-minute timer before opening social media

Your space

A notebook by the keyboard for urges

2

Make time for traction3,4,7

Plan your values into your calendar

You can't call something a distraction unless you know what you meant to do instead. 3

How to design for it

  • Timebox the week across three domains: You, Relationships and Work 3
  • Schedule sync: agree your calendar with the people who depend on your time 4,7
Your day

Gym and dinner with friends booked like meetings

Your work

Share your timeboxed week with your manager

Your space

A weekly plan on the fridge

3

Hack back external triggers3,4,7

Is this trigger serving me, or am I serving it?

Pings, dings and interruptions are useful only if they lead to traction. 3

How to design for it

  • Phone: remove, replace, rearrange and reclaim 3
  • Meetings: no agenda, no meeting 7,8
  • Group chat: get in and get out, like a sauna 3,9
  • Signal focus: a screen sign, or a light-up “concentration crown” like the one Eyal's wife wears 3,10
Your day

Notifications off for everything but people

Your work

Scheduled office hours for questions

Your space

A sign on your monitor during focus time

4

Prevent distraction with pacts3,4

Decide in advance

Precommitments as a last line of defence. 3

How to design for it

  • Effort pact: add friction to distraction 3
  • Price pact: put money at stake 3
  • Identity pact: see yourself as someone who is indistractable 3
Your day

A site blocker you can't easily turn off

Your work

Pay a friend if you skip the writing block

Your space

Phone in a timed lockbox

System

Design to Hack Distraction By Design

Five steps for designing a focused space, team or day, built on Eyal's model.

  1. Identify external triggersAudit what pulls attention away: physical (layout, clutter, movement), digital (screens, notifications) and social (interruptions, open access). 3
  2. Remove or reduce triggersFocus zones, minimal setups and blocked distractions. Goal: less cognitive tax. 3
  3. Protect with social structureTimeboxed focus hours, office hours for questions, no agenda no meeting, and get-in-get-out group chat. Goal: lower interruption cost. 3,7,8
  4. Replace with designed cuesVisual and auditory signals for focus time, like a screen sign or a concentration crown. Goal: channel attention on purpose. 3,10
  5. Reinforce focusMultichannel multitasking (pair low-attention tasks, like walking while listening) and temptation bundling. Goal: sustained effort. 3,11,12

Example: A shared studio

  • Identify: foot traffic past the desks, a TV, constant Slack pings
  • Remove: desks turned away from the walkway, TV off until 4 pm
  • Protect: focus mornings, questions at 2 pm office hours
  • Cue: a lamp on means “don't interrupt”
  • Reinforce: walking 1:1s and a shared playlist for admin hour

Reflect

What feeling usually comes right before you get distracted?

Which trigger around you is serving someone else's goals, not yours?

Practice 3 days3

  1. Keep a Distraction Tracker for three days: each time you get distracted, note the time and the feeling just before.
  2. Look for patterns: which feelings and triggers come up most?
  3. Pick one fix for the most common one: the 10-minute rule, a removed notification, or a pact.
  4. Timebox next week across You, Relationships and Work.

In Sedi: Timebox the week in Timely's day view, including blocks for yourself and your people, not just work.

Lesson 03 · Habit formation

Atomic Habits

James Clear · 2018

An Easy & Proven Way to Build Good Habits & Break Bad Ones: how tiny changes compound into remarkable results, and how to design the cues, cravings and rewards behind every habit. 1,2

James Clear writes about habits, decision-making and continuous improvement at jamesclear.com. 3

⚛️Atomic HabitsJames Clear

Principle

Design the system, not the goal

Clear argues that goals set direction but systems make progress. You don't rise to the level of your goals; you fall to the level of your systems. Small improvements of about 1% compound over time, even when the results stay invisible for a long while (his Plateau of Latent Potential). 2,4,11

Framework

The Four Laws of Behavior Change2

Every habit runs through a loop of cue → craving → response → reward. Each law shapes one stage. To break a bad habit, invert the law. 2

1

Make it obvious2,5,6

Cue · invert: make it invisible

Habits start with a cue you notice. Make the cue for a good habit unmissable and hide the cues for bad ones. 2

How to design for it

  • Write an implementation intention: “I will [behavior] at [time] in [location]” 6
  • Use the habit stacking formula: “After [current habit], I will [new habit]” 6
  • Put the cue in plain sight, like the book on your pillow 2
Your day

Running shoes by the door

Your work

Tomorrow's first task written on a sticky note

Your space

Fruit on the counter, sweets in a high cupboard

2

Make it attractive2,7

Craving · invert: make it unattractive

The more appealing a habit, the more likely you'll do it. 2

How to design for it

  • Temptation bundling: only do something you want while doing something you need to 7,12
  • Join a group where the behavior you want is normal 2
Your day

Favourite podcast only on the treadmill

Your work

Good coffee only during deep-work blocks

Your space

A running club that meets at a café

3

Make it easy2,8

Response · invert: make it difficult

Reduce the friction between you and the good behavior, and add friction to the bad. 2

How to design for it

  • The Two-Minute Rule: scale a new habit down to a version that takes under two minutes 8
  • Prepare your environment so the next action is ready 2
Your day

“Read one page” instead of “read more”

Your work

Open the doc before you close your laptop

Your space

Phone charger in the kitchen, not the bedroom

4

Make it satisfying2,9,10

Reward · invert: make it unsatisfying

We repeat what feels good right away. 2

How to design for it

  • Track the habit so progress is visible 9
  • Never miss twice: one slip is an accident, two starts a pattern 9
  • For bad habits, a habit contract with an accountability partner adds an immediate cost 10
Your day

A tick on the calendar each morning

Your work

Close the day by logging what you shipped

Your space

A jar that fills with a marble per workout

Note: Clear frames habits as votes for an identity. Decide who you want to be, then prove it with small wins. Change works best from identity outward, not outcomes inward. 4

System

The Habit Design Card By Design

Run one habit through Clear's tools in order.

  1. Score your dayList your daily habits and mark each +, – or = for the person you want to be (Clear's Habits Scorecard). 5
  2. Choose the identityFinish the sentence: “I'm the kind of person who…” 4
  3. Stack itAfter [current habit], I will [new habit]. 6
  4. Shrink itCut it to a two-minute starting version. 8
  5. Track itMark it daily, and never miss twice. 9

Example: Becoming a writer

  • Score: scrolling at breakfast is a “–”
  • Identity: I'm someone who writes every day
  • Stack: after I pour my first coffee, I'll open my notebook
  • Shrink: write one sentence
  • Track: a tick on the calendar, and never two blanks in a row

Reflect

Which of your current habits are votes for the person you want to become, and which are votes against?

If you only fall to the level of your systems, what does your current system produce?

Practice 20 min5,6,8

  1. Do the Habits Scorecard: list everything you do from waking up, and mark each item +, – or =. Don't change anything yet.
  2. Choose one “–” to weaken and one new habit to build.
  3. Write the new habit as a stack: “After I…, I will…”.
  4. Shrink it to two minutes and do it once today.

Lesson 04 · Behavioral economics

Nudge

Richard H. Thaler and Cass R. Sunstein · 2008

Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness: how the way choices are presented shapes what people choose, and how to design those choices so people are better off by their own standards. 1,2

Richard H. Thaler won the 2017 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences; Cass R. Sunstein is a legal scholar at Harvard. 2,11

👉NudgeRichard H. Thaler and Cass R. Sunstein

Principle

There is no neutral design

Someone always decides how options are laid out: what comes first, what's the default, what's easy. Thaler and Sunstein call that person a choice architect. Since design always influences the choice, design it on purpose to help people while leaving every option open. They call this libertarian paternalism. 2,3 They also contrast Econs, the perfectly rational people of economic models, with Humans, who think with a fast Automatic System and a slow Reflective System and make predictable mistakes. 2,8

Framework

NUDGES4

The authors' checklist of choice-architecture tools. Each letter is one lever. 4

1

iNcentives4

Make costs and benefits salient

People respond to incentives they notice. Make the real costs and benefits visible at the moment of choice. 4

How to design for it

  • Show the cost where the decision happens 4
Physical

Energy meters that show cost per hour

Digital

Price per month shown next to price per year

Experience

Showing water saved per shorter shower

2

Understand mappings4

Connect choices to outcomes

Help people see how a choice translates into results they care about. 4

How to design for it

  • Translate options into outcomes, e.g. megapixels into print sizes 4
Physical

Paint swatches shown on a real wall

Digital

A plan picker framed by “what you'll use”

Experience

A taster before choosing a course

3

Defaults2,4,5,10

What happens if you do nothing

Defaults are powerful because many people stick with them. Automatic enrolment in retirement savings is the classic case. 2,5 The book also describes Save More Tomorrow, where people commit now to save more from future raises. 2,10

How to design for it

  • Set the default to the option most people would choose on reflection 2
Physical

Healthy food at eye level in the cafeteria

Digital

Automatic enrolment in a pension plan

Experience

Green energy as the default tariff

4

Give feedback4

Tell people how they're doing

Good systems let people know when they're doing well and when they're making mistakes. 4

How to design for it

  • Make feedback immediate and visible 4
Physical

Speed signs that show your speed

Digital

A warning before sending without the attachment

Experience

A progress bar through a long form

5

Expect error4

Design for human mistakes

People make predictable mistakes, so design forgives them. A forcing function is the classic example: the cash machine that gives your card back before your cash. 4

How to design for it

  • Add forcing functions for the most common mistake 4
Physical

A fuel nozzle that won't fit the wrong tank

Digital

Undo instead of “Are you sure?”

Experience

A checklist before the event starts

6

Structure complex choices4

Simplify when options multiply

As choices grow, people need help narrowing them, such as filtering by what matters most or recommendations from similar people. 4

How to design for it

  • Offer filters and sensible groupings 4
Physical

A wine shelf sorted by taste, not region

Digital

Filters for the three features people care about

Experience

A set menu alongside the full menu

Note: Nudge: The Final Edition (2021) adds a chapter on sludge, the friction that makes good choices harder, plus Smart Disclosure and choice engines. 6,7,9

System

Nudge Yourself By Design

Use the book's tools as your own choice architect.

  1. Audit your defaultsList five recurring choices and what happens if you do nothing. 2,4
  2. Reset themChange each default to what you'd choose on reflection. 2
  3. Hunt the sludgeRemove a step from a good habit you keep skipping. 6
  4. Add a forcing functionMake your most common mistake hard to make. 4
  5. Close the loopAdd immediate feedback so you see progress. 4

Example: Saving money

  • Default: your pay goes straight to your current account
  • Reset: 10% moves to savings automatically on payday
  • Sludge: delete saved cards from shopping sites
  • Forcing function: a 48-hour rule before any purchase over $100
  • Feedback: a weekly glance at the savings balance

Reflect

Where in your life are you living with a default you never chose?

If you're always a choice architect for someone, whose choices do you shape?

Practice 30 min2,4,6

  1. List five recurring choices: savings, subscriptions, your phone's home screen, snacks, calendar invites.
  2. For each, write what happens if you do nothing.
  3. Change at least two defaults to what you'd pick on reflection.
  4. Time each step of one good habit you keep skipping, and remove one.

Lesson 05 · Positive psychology

Feel-Good Productivity

Ali Abdaal · 2023

A case that feeling good is the engine of productivity, not the reward for it, built on research from psychology and neuroscience. 1,2

Ali Abdaal trained and worked as a doctor in the UK before building one of YouTube's most-watched channels on productivity. 11

☀️Feel-Good ProductivityAli Abdaal

Principle

Design for good feelings first

Abdaal argues that the discipline-and-pressure approach has it backwards. Positive feelings raise energy, energy makes work easier to start and sustain, and the work then feels better still. So instead of forcing output, change how the work feels. 1,2,3

Framework

Energise · Unblock · Sustain1,2

The book is built in three parts: what gives us energy, what blocks us from starting, and what keeps us from burning out. Each chapter ends with small experiments to try. 1,2

1

Energise1,2,3

Play, Power and People

Three sources of good feeling. Play: bringing lightness and curiosity to a task. Power: a sense of confidence, competence and control. People: feeling part of something with others. 2,3

How to design for it

  • Ask of a dull task: what would this look like if it were fun? 3
  • Choose a character to play while you work, and allow yourself side quests that follow your curiosity 4,12
  • Build a sense of power by teaching what you're learning (the protégé effect) and reframing “I have to” as “I get to” 5,13
  • Treat collaborators as comrades and over-communicate 3,4
Your day

A playlist and a timer turn chores into a race

Your work

Explain a new skill to a colleague to lock it in

Your space

A shared table where people work alongside each other

Open the doc and write for five minutes, then decide

One evening a week with nothing scheduled

Your work

Cap active projects at five, park the rest

Your space

A hobby corner that's always ready to use

Redesign before you resign

The Art of Gathering

Principle

Design for a purpose, not a category

“Dinner party”, “meeting” and “birthday” are formats, not reasons. Parker argues that most gatherings disappoint because we copy the format and skip the purpose. A good purpose is specific and even disputable: reasonable people could disagree with it. 2,3

Framework

The arc of a gathering2,4

The book follows a gathering from first idea to final goodbye. Five of its moves: 2,4

1

Decide why2,3

A specific, disputable purpose

How to design for it

  • Ask why three times until the purpose is something someone could disagree with 2
Physical

A dinner “to welcome the new neighbours into the street”

Digital

A call “to decide, not to update”

Experience

A birthday “to thank the people who got me through this year”

2

Close doors2

Exclude with generosity

The guest list is the first test of your purpose. Who to leave out, the size of the group and the venue all shape what can happen. 2

How to design for it

  • Invite only the people who serve the purpose 2
Physical

A room just big enough to feel full

Digital

Six people on the call, not twenty

Experience

No plus-ones for a reunion dinner

3

Generous authority2,4

Don't be a chill host

Hosts who stay hands-off leave guests unprotected. Use your authority generously: protect guests, connect them, and treat them as equals. 2,4

How to design for it

  • Introduce people, step in when someone dominates, and set the tone 4
Physical

Seating planned so strangers meet

Digital

The host calls on quiet voices by name

Experience

Gently cutting off the long speech

4

Pop-up rules4

A temporary alternative world

Explicit, temporary rules that hold only for this gathering replace unspoken etiquette and let people from different backgrounds play the same game. 4

How to design for it

  • Set two or three simple rules, like no phones or no job titles 4
Physical

Phones in a basket at the door

Digital

Cameras on, no multitasking, 45 minutes

Experience

No talking about work until dessert

5

Open and close with intention2,5,6

How to design for it

  • Send a note beforehand that sets the mood 5
  • End with a closing ritual, not an announcement 6
Physical

A moment of silence before the meal

Digital

One word from everyone to close the call

Experience

A postcard mailed to guests a week later

Lesson 14 · Social psychology

The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations

Fritz Heider · 1958

A founding text of social psychology. Heider studied “naive psychology”: the common-sense theories everyone uses to explain why other people do what they do. 1,2

Fritz Heider (1896–1988) was an Austrian-born psychologist whose work on attribution and balance shaped modern social psychology. 2

🤝The Psychology of Interpersonal RelationsFritz Heider

Principle

Design for how people explain each other

We constantly guess at the causes of other people's behavior, and those guesses shape how we treat them. Heider noticed we lean toward explaining others' actions by who they are rather than by the situation they're in. Later researchers named that tilt the fundamental attribution error. 2,3

Framework

Attribution and Balance2

Two of Heider's ideas that are still used everywhere from design to diplomacy. 2

1

Attribution2,3

Person or situation?

When someone acts, we explain it either by something inside them (traits, motives) or something outside (the circumstances). We tend to over-weight the inside explanation for others, especially when the behavior annoys us. 2,3

How to design for it

  • Before judging, list situational reasons someone might act this way 2
Your day

The driver who cut you off may be rushing to hospital

Your work

The late reply may come from an overloaded week, not disrespect

Your space

The messy kitchen may come from a broken dishwasher

2

Balance2

We like our relationships consistent

People prefer their likes and dislikes to fit together: my friend's friend should be my friend, and if I like you and you like an idea, I'm pulled to like it too. An unbalanced triangle creates tension we try to resolve. 2

How to design for it

  • When you feel tension in a relationship, map the triangle and see which link is out of balance 2
Your day

A partner who loves a friend you find difficult

Your work

A manager you trust backing a plan you doubt

Your space

Housemates split over a shared rule

Note: Heider built on an earlier experiment with Marianne Simmel, in which people watching simple moving shapes described them as having intentions and personalities. 2,4

System

The Charitable Explanation By Design

A short routine for moments of friction with someone.

  1. Write your first explanationWhat do you think caused their behavior? 2
  2. Flip itWrite three situational explanations instead. 2,3
  3. Map the triangleYou, them and the issue: which link is negative? 2
  4. Choose a responseAct on the most charitable explanation that's still plausible. 2
  5. Check itAsk, don't assume. 2

Example: A colleague who keeps missing deadlines

  • First explanation: they don't care
  • Flip: too many projects, unclear priorities, something at home
  • Triangle: you both care about the launch, but disagree about the timeline
  • Response: offer to agree on priorities together
  • Check: ask what's getting in the way

Reflect

Whose behavior have you been explaining by their character rather than their circumstances?

Which relationship triangle in your life feels out of balance?

Practice 15 min By Design

  1. Think of someone whose behavior bothers you.
  2. Write your explanation, then three situational ones.
  3. Draw the triangle of you, them and the issue, and mark each link + or −.
  4. Decide on one conversation or one change of view.
