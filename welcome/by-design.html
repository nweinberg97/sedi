Lesson 01 · Behavior design

Tiny Habits

BJ Fogg · 2019

A practical method for changing behavior by starting very small, from the founder of Stanford's Behavior Design Lab.

BJ Fogg, PhD, founded the Behavior Design Lab at Stanford University.

🌱Tiny HabitsBJ Fogg

Principle

Design for low motivation

Motivation rises and falls through the day. When we're tired, it's the first thing to go. A behavior designed to work on your lowest-energy day is far more likely to happen: make it easy, make it visible, anchor it, and make it feel rewarding.

Framework

The Fogg Behavior Model

B = MAP

A behavior happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt come together at the same moment. To design for a behavior, pull on those three levers.

1

Motivation

How much you want to do it

Shaped by what you already want, by rewards, by consequences, and by what people around you are doing.

How to design for it

  • Celebrate right after the behavior so it feels good
  • Match people with behaviors they already want
Physical

Confetti at the marathon finish line

Digital

A badge after someone's first contribution

Experience

Stairs that play a note with every step

2

Ability

How easy it is to do

Behaviors get hard through complexity, effort, unfamiliarity, unclear instructions, missing resources or social risk.

How to design for it

  • Make it less hard: remove friction, or make it the default
  • Make it easier: shrink the action or break it into smaller steps
Physical

Seats in small circles so talking is natural

Digital

A one-tap poll instead of a blank reply box

Experience

One-word intros to break the ice

3

Prompt

What reminds you to do it, right now

Fogg sorts prompts into three kinds: person prompts (a feeling or body signal), context prompts (something in the environment) and action prompts (a behavior you already do).

How to design for it

  • Anchor it: attach the new behavior to something people already do
  • Make it visible: put the cue where it can't be missed
Physical

A sign on the café door: “Joining the run?”

Digital

“How do you want to show up today?” right after login

Experience

Grab a coloured badge, find someone with the same colour

Note: Motivation is the least reliable lever. When designing, start with ability and prompts.

System

The Tiny Design Test

Run any idea through five levers.

  1. Make it easyShrink the action or lower the stakes.
  2. Make it less hardRemove effort and barriers.
  3. Make it visiblePut cues in the environment so it's always in view.
  4. Anchor a promptTie it to something people already do.
  5. Celebrate completionMake finishing feel rewarding.

Example: Café run club

  • Easy: shorten the run from 5 km to 1 km
  • Less hard: a QR code on the door for quick sign-up
  • Anchor: the sign on the exit reminds people as they leave
  • Visible: posters show the start time, route and social fun
  • Celebrate: a big group cheer at the finish

Reflect

How will you use this model to shape behavior for the better?

How could you design for joy? For calm? For connection?

Practice 30 min

  1. Choose one outcome to design for: joy, connection, calm, movement…
  2. Pick a project or a personal habit you want to shape toward it.
  3. Run it through one lever of the Tiny Design Test.
  4. Share your design with someone and ask where they'd still feel friction.

In Sedi: Write the habit as a recipe (“After I…, I will…”) and add it as a Routine in Timely. The check sound when you tick it is your celebration.
